# Slide 2 — The Idea / Proposed Solution

## Title

Unlike slides 3-5, this slide's title is **not** a fixed label like "IDEA." It's the project/solution's own name or a short descriptive title of what's being built — e.g. a coined product name, or a plain description like "Automatic Fabric Inspection System for Textile Industry." Pick (or ask the user for) a short, memorable name if they don't already have one; it does double duty as branding throughout the rest of the deck.

## Two structural variants — pick based on what the idea *is*

Across real decks, this slide takes one of two shapes depending on whether the idea centers on physical hardware/a tangible prototype, or is a pure software/data/service product. Don't force the wrong shape onto an idea — it will feel hollow (a flowchart with nothing to actually flow, or a feature-grid for something that's fundamentally one physical device).

### Variant A — Problem list + solution grid (software, platforms, services)

Best for apps, AI platforms, marketplaces, advisory tools — anything where the value is a bundle of features/capabilities rather than a single physical build.

1. **Challenges and Problems** — a bulleted list of 4-7 problems, each opening with a bold problem-name lead phrase, colon, then a one-line explanation of the pain point. (Bold-lead-phrase formula — see `writing-style.md`.)
2. **Proposed Solution** — 2-4 named sub-solutions, each a bold sub-header (optionally prefixed with a ➤ arrow or number) followed by 1-3 sentences of prose, with the 3-6 most important differentiating phrases bolded/colored inline within that prose.
3. **Innovation and Uniqueness** — usually the most visually distinct block: a grid of 4-6 colored rounded boxes/pills, each with a bold 2-4 word title and a 1-2 line description. This is often positioned to one side (right column) as a standalone visual feature, separate from the prose sections.
4. **How it Addresses the Problems** — maps solution back to each named problem, same bold-lead-phrase-then-explanation format, ideally color-matched or ordered to mirror the problem list so a reviewer can trace cause → fix at a glance.

An alternative to the grid for "Proposed Solution" specifically: a **donut/segmented diagram** with a central label (the platform name) and 4-6 labeled capability segments radiating outward — use this when the solution really is best understood as a bundle of interlocking capabilities rather than a numbered list of named sub-solutions.

### Variant B — Process flow + real photos (hardware, IoT, physical prototypes)

Best for anything with an actual physical build: sensors, cameras, machines, wearables, robotics.

1. **Proposed Solution as a flowchart** — 3-5 connected rounded-rectangle boxes with arrows, showing the physical/technical sequence end to end (e.g. capture → detect → act → report). This usually anchors the slide visually rather than sitting in a column of text.
2. **Problem Statement** — a short prose paragraph, with 2 real photographs of the problem in its current (manual/unsolved) form, captioned plainly. Real photos here do real credibility work — always include them if the user has any.
3. **How it Addresses the Problem** — a checkmark (✅) or diamond (❖) bulleted list, bold-lead-phrase formula, each line naming a concrete capability the physical system delivers.
4. **Innovation and Uniqueness** — same bulleted format, focused on what's technically novel about the build (e.g. a particular sensor fusion approach, a specific model architecture choice, a form factor advantage).

## What both variants share

- A short (2-4 sentence) problem-framing paragraph somewhere on the slide, with inline bold/colored emphasis on the 4-8 most important phrases.
- At least one strongly visual element beyond plain text — either the innovation grid, the flowchart, the donut diagram, or real photos. Never ship this slide as pure paragraphs and bullets.
- The header/footer/color-coding conventions from `design-system.md` apply exactly as on every other slide.

## Ask the user, if you don't already know

- Is there a real, physical prototype, or is this a software/service concept? (Decides the variant.)
- What are the 4-7 sharpest pain points this solves — ideally each nameable in 2-4 words?
- What makes this specifically different from an obvious/existing approach to the same problem? (Feeds Innovation and Uniqueness — generic answers here are the most common way this slide ends up weak.)
