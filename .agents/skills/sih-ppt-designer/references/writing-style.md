# Writing Style — How SIH Decks Actually Sound

Read this before drafting any bullet, card, or paragraph. The wording matters as much as the layout — a well-designed slide with generic, unquantified text still reads as amateur, and a plainly-laid-out slide with sharp, specific wording still reads as credible.

## The bold-lead-phrase formula

This is the dominant sentence pattern for essentially every bullet across every category of every slide type:

```
**Bold lead phrase** [ : or → ] explanation clause.
```

- **Lead phrase**: 2-5 words, a noun phrase naming the concept/feature/benefit — not a sentence. Examples of the *shape*: "Government Schemes", "Reduced Electricity Bills", "Blockchain Trust System", "Real-Time Quality Checks".
- **Separator**: a colon is more common overall; an arrow (→) shows up in the more polished, persuasive decks and reads slightly more like "cause → effect" than "term: definition". Either is authentic to the format — **pick one and use it consistently for the whole deck** rather than mixing both.
- **Explanation clause**: short — a fragment or one plain sentence, not a paragraph. Concrete and specific; ideally carries a number, a named tool, or a named entity rather than a vague generality.

Illustrative examples of the pattern in use (adapt the specifics to the idea at hand, don't reuse these verbatim):

- "Government Schemes: Central/state tourism development funds."
- "Blockchain Trust System: Secure payments, verified providers, and digital certification for fair trade."
- "Reduced Electricity Bills → Smarter load management and storage optimisation cuts costs."
- "Real-Time Quality Checks: Problems are addressed before they worsen because flaws are identified immediately during production."
- "Phased Rollout: Initiate in key hotspot districts before statewide expansion to reduce upfront costs."

## Verb choices

Lean on active, outcome-driven verbs: Boosts, Reduces, Ensures, Enables, Empowers, Prevents, Builds, Supports, Promotes, Increases, Cuts, Saves, Strengthens. Avoid passive or vague verb phrases like "is used for," "helps with," "deals with" — they read as filler next to the punchier style everywhere else on the slide.

## Quantify everything you can

A bare claim ("saves time," "reduces cost") is always weaker than the same claim with a number attached, even a rough or estimated one:

- Percentages: "20-30% reduction in grid electricity bills."
- Currency: "$45-50 Billion total addressable market," "₹12,950/day potential revenue."
- Counts and reach: "34.6 kg of plastic waste collected in a week," "10.6 million children affected."
- Time: "cuts inspection time from hours to minutes."

If the user hasn't given you real figures, ask for them rather than inventing precise-looking numbers — a specific-looking but fabricated statistic is worse than an honest range or no number at all. It's fine to draft with clearly-marked placeholder figures (e.g. "[X]% reduction") if the user explicitly wants a draft to fill in later.

## Specific nouns beat generic ones

Name the actual framework, cloud provider, or API instead of "a database" or "cloud services." Name the actual ministry, scheme, or organization instead of "government support." This is visible everywhere technology stacks are shown as real product logos (Python, React, MongoDB, AWS, TensorFlow) rather than a text list of categories, and wherever funding sources are named specifically (Digital India, UNESCO, state tourism boards) rather than just "grants."

## Where prose survives — and where it doesn't

- The one or two **problem-statement** and **solution-overview** blocks per slide are written as 2-4 sentence prose paragraphs, with 4-8 key phrases bolded and colored inline for skimmability.
- Almost everything downstream of that — innovation points, benefit lists, strategy lists, impact lists — drops straight to the bold-lead-phrase bullet fragment. Don't write full explanatory sentences in these sections; if a bullet is running past ~20 words, it's probably still a prose paragraph pretending to be a bullet.

## Section headers

Short (2-4 words), Title Case or ALL CAPS, colored to match that section's assigned category color from the design system. E.g. "Technical Feasibility," "Innovation and Uniqueness," "Business Model."

## Credibility markers belong in the wording, not just the visuals

When the user has any of the following, say it plainly and place it where it'll be seen (usually near the tech stack in Technical Approach, or near the closing of Impact and Benefits): competition or award wins (name the award/track), an industry mentor or referral (name + company + years of experience + contact), a working prototype or demo link, or field-test/pilot results. Reviewers weight demonstrated traction heavily, and stating it directly ("IEEE YESIST12 2025 Winner — FET100 Track") reads as far stronger than folding it into generic language.
